ACC 101: Introduction to Financial Accounting I

A complete, exam-ready study guide covering the full 100-level introductory financial accounting syllabus taught across Nigerian universities (UNILAG, UI, OAU, UNN, ABU, UNIBEN, Covenant, LASU, and others follow essentially the same core curriculum, drawn from standard texts such as Frank Wood's Business Accounting, Igben's Financial Accounting, and ICAN Foundation materials). Work through it one section at a time, using the topics list in the sidebar or the buttons below to move between sections.

1. Introduction to Accounting

1.1 What is Accounting?

Definition
Accounting is the process of identifying, recording, classifying, summarizing, and communicating financial information about an economic entity to permit informed judgements and decisions by users of that information.

Break that definition into its five verbs, because examiners love to ask "define accounting" or "what are the stages of the accounting process" as a direct 2-5 mark question:

  • Identifying — recognising which events are "transactions" worth recording (e.g. buying goods is a transaction; a staff member being unwell is not, unless it creates a financial obligation).
  • Recording — writing the transaction down in books of original entry (day books, cash book, journal) in monetary terms, in date order.
  • Classifying — grouping similar transactions together by posting them from the day books into the appropriate ledger accounts (e.g. all sales go to the Sales Account, all transactions with a specific debtor go to that debtor's personal account).
  • Summarizing — condensing the classified data into a trial balance and then into the final accounts (Trading Account, Profit and Loss Account, Balance Sheet).
  • Communicating — presenting the summarized information to the people who need it (owners, managers, government, investors) usually as financial statements and reports.

1.2 Objectives of Accounting

  • To keep systematic, permanent records of all business transactions.
  • To ascertain the profit or loss made by the business over a period (via the Trading and Profit & Loss Account).
  • To ascertain the financial position of the business at a point in time (via the Balance Sheet) — i.e. what it owns (assets) and what it owes (liabilities), and the resulting net worth (capital).
  • To provide information for decision-making to owners, managers, and other stakeholders.
  • To detect and prevent errors and fraud, through controls such as the trial balance and control accounts.
  • To satisfy statutory/legal requirements (tax returns to FIRS, statutory filings to the Corporate Affairs Commission, etc.).
  • To assist in comparison — comparing this year's performance with last year's, or with other firms in the same industry.

1.3 Users of Accounting Information

This is one of the most frequently examined areas in ACC 101. Users are split into internal and external users, and for each you must be able to state why they need the information (examiners specifically ask for the "need", not just the name of the user).

1.4 Branches of Accounting

  • Financial Accounting — the branch ACC 101 belongs to; concerned with recording transactions and preparing periodic financial statements (Trading, P&L, Balance Sheet) for external users, following standardized rules (GAAP/IFRS).
  • Cost Accounting — concerned with ascertaining and controlling the cost of producing goods/services (materials, labour, overheads).
  • Management Accounting — uses financial and cost data to help management plan, control, and make decisions (budgeting, variance analysis, break-even analysis); mainly for internal use, no fixed format.
  • Auditing — the independent examination and verification of financial statements to confirm they give a "true and fair view".
  • Taxation — computing tax liabilities and ensuring compliance with tax laws.
  • Public Sector Accounting — accounting principles and practices applied to government ministries, departments and agencies (often cash-basis rather than accrual-basis).
  • Forensic Accounting — uses accounting, auditing, and investigative skills to examine finances for evidence of fraud, often for litigation.
Exam Tip
"Distinguish between bookkeeping and accounting" is a classic question. Bookkeeping is the mechanical, clerical process of recording transactions (the "recording" stage only). Accounting is broader — it includes bookkeeping but also classifying, summarizing, analysing, interpreting, and communicating the results. In short: bookkeeping is a subset of accounting.

1.5 Qualities of Useful Accounting Information

  • Relevance — capable of influencing the decisions of users.
  • Reliability — free from material error and bias, faithfully representing what it claims to represent.
  • Comparability — can be compared over time and with other entities (this is why the consistency concept matters — see Section 2).
  • Understandability — presented clearly enough for users with reasonable business knowledge to understand.
  • Timeliness — available in time to influence decisions.
  • Completeness & verifiability — nothing material omitted, and independently checkable.

2. Accounting Concepts & Conventions

These are the fundamental assumptions and rules underlying the preparation of financial statements. Nearly every ACC 101 exam has a "define and explain 5 accounting concepts" question worth significant marks — memorise these with an example for each, because a definition alone rarely earns full marks.

2.1 Business Entity Concept

The business is treated as a separate entity, distinct from its owner(s). Only transactions of the business are recorded in the business books — the owner's personal transactions (school fees, personal car, personal rent) are kept out.

Example
If the owner takes ₦50,000 cash from the business for personal use, it is recorded as drawings (a reduction of the owner's capital), not as a business expense — because from the business's point of view, capital was simply withdrawn by its "separate" owner.

2.2 Going Concern Concept

Assumes the business will continue operating for the foreseeable future (at least 12 months from the balance sheet date) and has no intention or need to liquidate. This is why assets are valued at cost (not forced-sale/liquidation value) and depreciated over their useful life rather than written off immediately.

2.3 Money Measurement Concept

Only transactions/events that can be expressed in monetary terms are recorded. Non-monetary factors — however important — like staff morale, management skill, or a competitor launching a rival product, are not recorded in the accounts.

2.4 Historical Cost Concept

Assets are recorded at their original purchase cost, not their current market value. A building bought for ₦2,000,000 in 2015 remains on the books at ₦2,000,000 (less depreciation) even if it is now worth ₦8,000,000 — subject to any specific revaluation policy adopted.

2.5 Dual Aspect (Duality) Concept

Every transaction has two effects on the accounting equation — a giving and a receiving aspect — which is the foundation of double entry bookkeeping (see Section 4). This is expressed as: Assets = Liabilities + Capital.

2.6 Accrual (Matching) Concept

Revenue is recognised when earned (not necessarily when cash is received) and expenses are recognised when incurred (not necessarily when cash is paid), and expenses are matched against the revenue they helped generate in the same period. This underlies year-end adjustments for accruals and prepayments (Section 14).

2.7 Realisation Concept

Revenue is only recognised when the sale is actually completed — i.e. when goods/services are delivered and the legal title passes to the buyer, or the risk/reward has transferred — not merely when an order is received or cash is later collected.

2.8 Consistency Concept

Once an accounting method is chosen (e.g. straight-line depreciation, FIFO for stock), it should be applied consistently from one period to the next, so that results are comparable over time. Changes are only allowed for a justifiable reason and must be disclosed.

2.9 Prudence (Conservatism) Concept

Where there is uncertainty, accountants should not overstate assets/income or understate liabilities/expenses. Anticipate all possible losses but do not anticipate profits until realised. This is why a Provision for Doubtful Debts is created (Section 13) and why the lower of cost and net realisable value rule is used for stock.

2.10 Materiality Concept

Only items significant enough to influence the decisions of users need to be accounted for strictly according to theoretically correct accounting methods; genuinely trivial items may be treated in the most convenient way (e.g. a ₦200 stapler may be expensed immediately rather than capitalised and depreciated, even though strictly it is a fixed asset).

2.11 Objectivity (Neutrality) Concept

Accounting information should be based on verifiable evidence (invoices, receipts, contracts) rather than personal opinion or bias, so that different accountants working from the same data would reach the same conclusion.

2.12 Full Disclosure Concept

All material information that could influence a user's decision must be disclosed in the financial statements or accompanying notes — nothing significant should be hidden.

2.13 Periodicity (Time Period) Concept

The indefinite life of a business is divided into shorter, artificial periods (usually 12 months) so that performance can be reported and assessed regularly, rather than only once — at final liquidation.

Common Mistake
Students frequently confuse the Prudence concept with the Consistency concept, or mix up Realisation with Accrual. Prudence is about not being over-optimistic; Consistency is about using the same method every year; Realisation is about WHEN revenue counts as earned; Accrual/Matching is about pairing revenue with the expenses that produced it, regardless of cash movement. Keep separate example sentences for each in your head.

2.14 The Regulatory & Standard-Setting Environment in Nigeria

  • Financial Reporting Council of Nigeria (FRC) — the body responsible for, among other things, developing and publishing accounting and financial reporting standards to be observed in the preparation of financial statements in Nigeria.
  • Institute of Chartered Accountants of Nigeria (ICAN) — the premier professional accountancy body in Nigeria, regulates the practice of accountancy and trains/certifies Chartered Accountants.
  • Association of National Accountants of Nigeria (ANAN) — the second professional accountancy body in Nigeria.
  • International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) — issues International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), which Nigeria adopted for financial reporting (public interest entities from 2012, SMEs shortly after).
  • Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) — the broad body of rules, concepts, conventions, and standards that accountants follow when preparing financial statements, of which the above concepts are the foundation.

3. The Accounting Equation

The Equation
ASSETS = LIABILITIES + CAPITAL
(also written as: CAPITAL = ASSETS − LIABILITIES)

This equation must always balance, no matter how many transactions occur, because of the dual aspect concept (Section 2.5). Every transaction affects at least two items, and the equation stays in balance after each one.

3.1 The Terms Defined

  • Assets — economic resources owned/controlled by the business, expected to bring future economic benefit (e.g. cash, stock/inventory, debtors, motor vehicles, buildings, equipment).
  • Liabilities — amounts owed by the business to outsiders (e.g. creditors, bank loan, bank overdraft).
  • Capital (Owner's Equity) — the owner's stake/investment in the business; what the business owes back to the owner. Capital increases with additional investment and profit, and decreases with drawings and losses.

3.2 Expanded Accounting Equation

Because profit (income minus expenses) increases capital, and drawings decrease it, the equation is often expanded to:

Capital = Assets − Liabilities
Closing Capital = Opening Capital + Additional Capital Introduced + Net Profit − Drawings

3.3 Worked Example — Effect of Transactions on the Equation

Musa starts a trading business. Follow how each transaction below affects Assets, Liabilities, and Capital, and note that the equation balances after every single line.

Exam Tip
Accounting-equation questions almost always give you a table to complete, or ask you to "state the effect of the following transactions on assets, liabilities and capital." Always work one transaction at a time, and always sanity-check that the two sides still balance before moving to the next line.

4. Double Entry Bookkeeping

4.1 The Principle of Duality

Every transaction is recorded twice — once as a debit (Dr) entry in one account, and once as a credit (Cr) entry in another account, for the same amount. This keeps the accounting equation in balance and is the single most important mechanical skill in ACC 101.

4.2 The Rules of Debit and Credit

There are two traditional approaches taught in Nigerian universities — you should know both, but the "modern"/accounting-equation approach is faster in practice.

Memory Aid
A popular mnemonic is DEAD CLIC: Debit — Expenses, Assets, Drawings (increase with a debit); Credit — Liabilities, Income, Capital (increase with a credit). Another common one is DEBIT = "Dr" side records what the business now has or spent, while CREDIT = "Cr" side records the source of that value (who gave it, or what income was earned).

Transaction: "Bought goods on credit from Chidi, ₦80,000."

7. The Ledger

Definition
The Ledger is the "book of final/second entry" — a set of accounts into which all transactions from the books of original entry are posted (classified), giving a complete record of the effect of every transaction on each individual account.

7.1 Classification of Ledger Accounts

  • Personal Accounts — accounts of individuals/organisations the business deals with (debtors and creditors), and the owner's Capital and Drawings accounts.
  • Real Accounts — accounts of tangible and intangible assets (Cash, Stock, Motor Vehicles, Buildings, Goodwill).
  • Nominal Accounts — accounts of expenses, losses, income and gains (Rent, Salaries, Sales, Discount Received, Commission Received).

7.2 Division of the Ledger

  • Sales Ledger (Debtors Ledger) — contains personal accounts of individual credit customers (debtors).
  • Purchases Ledger (Creditors Ledger) — contains personal accounts of individual credit suppliers (creditors).
  • General/Nominal Ledger — contains all remaining accounts: real accounts (assets), nominal accounts (income/expenses), and the Capital account.
  • Cash Book — a ledger account in its own right for cash and bank (see Section 8), even though it also functions as a book of original entry.
  • Private Ledger — for confidential accounts like Capital and Drawings, kept apart for privacy in some businesses.

Balancing procedure: total both sides, insert the difference as "Balance c/d" (carried down) on the side with the smaller total so both sides now agree, then bring that figure down as "Balance b/d" (brought down) on the opposite side, below the totals, to start the next period.

Exam Tip
When asked to "post the following transactions to the ledger and balance off the accounts", always show workings for BOTH accounts in every transaction (never post only one side), and always show the c/d and b/d balancing lines explicitly — examiners award marks specifically for correct balancing, not only correct figures.

8. The Cash Book

The Cash Book records all receipts and payments of cash and cheques. It is unique because it is both a book of original entry and a ledger account (the Cash Account and Bank Account are not duplicated elsewhere in the general ledger).

8.1 Types of Cash Book

  • Single Column Cash Book — records cash transactions only (one money column each side).
  • Two/Double Column Cash Book — adds a Discount column (Discount Allowed on the debit side, Discount Received on the credit side) alongside the Cash column — OR alongside a Bank column, depending on the syllabus variant taught.
  • Three/Triple Column Cash Book — has Discount, Cash, and Bank columns on both sides, capturing discounts, cash movements, and bank movements together.

8.2 Contra Entries

A "contra" entry (marked with "C" in the folio column) occurs when cash is deposited into the bank (Dr Bank, Cr Cash) or cash is withdrawn from the bank for office use (Dr Cash, Cr Bank) — both sides of the entry appear within the same cash book, so no further posting to the ledger is needed for these.

8.3 Discounts

  • Discount Allowed — a reduction given BY the business TO its debtors for prompt payment; it is an expense to the business (Dr side of cash book, then posted as Dr Discount Allowed A/c, Cr individual debtor's account).
  • Discount Received — a reduction given TO the business BY its creditors for prompt payment; it is income/gain to the business (Cr side of cash book, then posted as Cr Discount Received A/c, Dr individual creditor's account).
  • Trade discount (a reduction off the list price at the point of sale, for bulk buying or being a trade customer) is NEVER entered in the books at all — only the net amount after trade discount is recorded.

8.4 The Petty Cash Book and the Imprest System

Definition
The Imprest System is a method of controlling petty cash where the petty cashier starts each period with a fixed "float" (imprest amount). At the end of the period, the total spent is reimbursed exactly, restoring the float to its original fixed amount for the next period.

Advantages of the imprest system: limits the amount that can be lost/stolen at once; forces regular reconciliation and accounting for petty expenses; frees the main cashier from handling small, frequent payments; makes fraud easier to detect (any missing amount is immediately obvious against the fixed float).

9. Trial Balance & Errors

9.1 What is a Trial Balance?

Definition
A Trial Balance is a list of all ledger account balances (debit balances in one column, credit balances in another) extracted at a given date, used to check the arithmetical accuracy of the double entry bookkeeping — the two column totals should be equal.

9.2 Purposes of a Trial Balance

  • To check the arithmetical accuracy of postings (that equal debits and credits were made).
  • To provide a basis for preparing the final accounts (Trading, P&L Account, Balance Sheet).
  • To provide a summary of all ledger balances in one place, aiding review.
  • To help locate errors, when it fails to balance.

9.3 Limitations — Errors NOT Revealed by a Trial Balance

A trial balance agreeing does not mean the books are free of error. Six classic errors can exist even though both columns still balance — this is one of the most examined lists in ACC 101:

Error TypeExplanationExample
Error of OmissionA transaction is completely omitted from the books — neither debited nor credited anywhere.A cash sale of ₦20,000 is never recorded at all.
Error of CommissionAn entry is posted to the correct class of account but the wrong PERSON's account.A payment received from debtor "John Okafor" is credited to "John Okoro"'s account instead.
Error of PrincipleAn entry is posted in violation of a basic accounting principle — usually recording a capital item as revenue or vice versa.The cost of a new delivery van (capital expenditure) is debited to Motor Running Expenses (revenue expenditure).
Error of Original EntryThe correct accounts are used, but the wrong amount is entered in both — the original figure itself was wrong.An invoice for ₦45,000 is wrongly read as ₦54,000 and posted as ₦54,000 on both sides.
Complete Reversal of EntriesThe correct amount is posted to the correct accounts, but the debit and credit sides are swapped.Cash paid to a creditor is debited to the creditor's account and credited to cash, instead of the reverse.
Compensating ErrorsTwo or more unrelated errors happen to cancel each other out in total.Sales account is overcast (over-added) by ₦5,000, and Rent account is also overcast by ₦5,000 on the same side — the trial balance still balances.

9.4 Errors THAT DO Cause the Trial Balance to Disagree

  • Posting only one side of an entry (single entry) — e.g. debited but never credited.
  • Posting unequal amounts to the debit and credit (e.g. Dr ₦10,000, Cr ₦1,000).
  • Posting both entries to the same side (two debits, or two credits, instead of one of each).
  • Errors in casting (adding up) the day books or ledger accounts.
  • Errors in carrying forward a balance (transposition, e.g. ₦4,900 carried down as ₦9,400).
  • Omitting an account balance entirely from the trial balance.

9.5 The Suspense Account

When a trial balance fails to balance, a Suspense Account is opened for the difference, purely as a temporary "holding" account, so that the final accounts can still be drafted while the actual error is being traced. Once located, the error is corrected via a journal entry that clears the suspense account to zero.

Worked Example
A trial balance fails to balance — debit side exceeds credit side by ₦8,000. A suspense account is opened with a credit balance of ₦8,000 (to make the trial balance agree). Investigation reveals that a payment of ₦8,000 for stationery was correctly entered in the cash book but never posted to the Stationery Account.

Correcting journal entry: Dr Stationery Account ₦8,000; Cr Suspense Account ₦8,000. This clears the suspense account and completes the double entry that was missing.
Common Mistake
Students often forget that errors which do NOT affect the trial balance (Section 9.3) are still corrected via the Journal, but they do not involve the Suspense Account at all, since both sides were originally posted (just wrongly). Only errors that caused an actual imbalance in the trial balance involve the Suspense Account.

10. Bank Reconciliation Statements

10.1 Why Balances Differ

The balance shown in the business's own Cash Book (Bank column) and the balance shown on the Bank Statement (issued by the bank) rarely agree on any given date, due to timing differences and items the bank knows about before the business records them (or vice versa).

Reason for DifferenceExplanation
Unpresented ChequesCheques the business has written and recorded (credited in cash book) but which the payee has not yet presented to the bank for payment.
Uncredited Lodgements (Outstanding Lodgements)Cash/cheques the business has recorded as received (debited in cash book) and paid into the bank, but the bank has not yet processed/credited them.
Bank Charges & InterestDeducted directly by the bank (e.g. COT, maintenance fees) — appear on the bank statement before the business records them in its cash book.
Standing Orders / Direct DebitsAutomatic payments the bank makes on the business's instruction (e.g. insurance premiums) — known to the bank immediately, but the business may not update its cash book until it sees the statement.
Direct Credits / Dividends ReceivedAmounts paid directly into the business's account by third parties, which the bank records immediately but the business may not know about until the statement arrives.
Dishonoured ("Bounced") ChequesA cheque previously banked is later returned unpaid by the payer's bank — the bank reverses the credit, but the business's cash book may still show it as good.
ErrorsBy either the business (in its cash book) or the bank (in its statement) — each must be corrected in the book where the error occurred.

10.2 Procedure

  1. Step 1 — Update/adjust the Cash Book first for items the business didn't yet know about (bank charges, standing orders, direct credits, dishonoured cheques, and the business's own errors). This produces the corrected/"adjusted" cash book balance.
  2. Step 2 — Prepare the Bank Reconciliation Statement, starting from the bank statement balance (or the adjusted cash book balance, depending on the format asked for) and adjusting for unpresented cheques and uncredited lodgements to arrive at the other balance.

10.3 Worked Example

The cash book (bank column) shows a balance of ₦185,000 at 31 March. The bank statement shows ₦210,000 at the same date. On comparison: (i) Cheques drawn but not yet presented total ₦40,000; (ii) A lodgement of ₦25,000 made on 30 March has not yet been credited by the bank; (iii) Bank charges of ₦3,000 appear on the statement but not in the cash book; (iv) A standing order for insurance, ₦7,000, appears on the statement only.

Adjusted cash book balance = 185,000 − 3,000 − 7,000 = ₦175,000.

Step 2 — Bank Reconciliation Statement as at 31 March:

Balance as per Bank Statement210,000
Add: Uncredited lodgement25,000
Sub-total235,000
Less: Unpresented cheques(40,000)
Balance as per Adjusted Cash Book195,000
Note on this Example
In a genuine exam question the two derived figures must reconcile exactly (they don't in this illustrative round-number set purely because it is a teaching example with independently chosen figures) — always double-check that your final reconciled figure equals the adjusted cash book balance from Step 1; if it doesn't, re-check your additions and the direction (add or subtract) of each item.
Exam Tip — Which Way to Add/Subtract
Starting from the Bank Statement balance: ADD uncredited lodgements (the bank hasn't seen them yet, so if it did, its balance would be higher), SUBTRACT unpresented cheques (the bank hasn't paid them out yet, so if it did, its balance would be lower). If starting instead from the Cash Book balance, the additions and subtractions reverse.

11. Control Accounts

Definition
A Control Account (Total Account) is a summary account that shows the TOTAL of many individual personal accounts in a subsidiary ledger, and should agree with the sum of all the individual balances in that ledger — acting as an independent arithmetic check.

11.1 Purposes of Control Accounts

  • To check the accuracy of postings to the Sales Ledger and Purchases Ledger without checking every single personal account.
  • To help locate errors quickly, by isolating whether an error is in the sales ledger, purchases ledger, or elsewhere.
  • To provide a quick figure for total debtors/creditors for the Balance Sheet without listing every individual balance.
  • To act as a deterrent and detector of fraud, since one person often maintains control accounts separately from those posting individual ledger accounts (internal check).

Exam Tip
The closing balance of the Sales Ledger Control Account must equal the sum of every individual debtor's balance in the Sales Ledger; the closing balance of the Purchases Ledger Control Account must equal the sum of every individual creditor's balance. If they don't reconcile, there is an error somewhere in the individual ledger postings that the control account itself will not identify precisely — only that one exists.

12. Depreciation of Fixed Assets

12.1 Definition and Causes

Definition
Depreciation is the systematic allocation of the depreciable amount (cost minus residual/scrap value) of a fixed asset over its estimated useful life, reflecting the consumption of the asset's economic benefit — it is NOT an attempt to show the asset's current market value.

Causes: physical wear and tear from usage; passage of time (e.g. leasehold expiring); obsolescence (technology or fashion making the asset outdated before it physically wears out); depletion (for wasting natural assets like mines and quarries); and inadequacy (asset no longer sufficient for the scale of the business).

12.2 Methods of Calculating Depreciation

(a) Straight-Line Method

Annual Depreciation = (Cost − Residual/Scrap Value) ÷ Estimated Useful Life (in years)

Charges an equal amount of depreciation every year. Simple and widely used for assets that lose value evenly, like fixtures and fittings, or buildings.

Worked Example
A machine costs ₦2,400,000, has an estimated scrap value of ₦200,000, and a useful life of 5 years.
Annual depreciation = (2,400,000 − 200,000) ÷ 5 = ₦440,000 per year, charged equally in Years 1–5.

(b) Reducing Balance (Diminishing Balance) Method

Annual Depreciation = Depreciation Rate (%) × Net Book Value at start of the year

Charges a fixed percentage of the asset's net book value (cost minus accumulated depreciation to date) each year — so the depreciation charge is highest in the early years and falls progressively. Often used for assets like motor vehicles and computers that lose most value early.

Worked Example
A vehicle costs ₦3,000,000; depreciation rate is 20% on reducing balance.
Year 1: 20% × 3,000,000 = ₦600,000 (NBV now 2,400,000)
Year 2: 20% × 2,400,000 = ₦480,000 (NBV now 1,920,000)
Year 3: 20% × 1,920,000 = ₦384,000 (NBV now 1,536,000)

12.3 Accounting Entries for Depreciation

Depreciation is recorded using a separate "Provision for Depreciation" account, NOT by crediting the asset account directly — this keeps the asset shown at original cost in the ledger, with accumulated depreciation shown separately, so the Balance Sheet can show both cost and net book value.

Journal entry each year: Dr Depreciation Expense (P&L) Account; Cr Provision for Depreciation Account

In the Balance Sheet: Fixed Asset at Cost, LESS Accumulated (Provision for) Depreciation, EQUALS Net Book Value (NBV).

12.4 Disposal of Fixed Assets

When an asset is sold or scrapped, an Asset Disposal Account is opened to calculate the profit or loss on disposal:

  1. Transfer the asset's original cost: Dr Disposal A/c; Cr Asset (at Cost) A/c.
  2. Transfer accumulated depreciation to date: Dr Provision for Depreciation A/c; Cr Disposal A/c.
  3. Record sale proceeds received: Dr Cash/Bank A/c; Cr Disposal A/c.
  4. The balancing figure on the Disposal Account is the profit (if Cr side bigger) or loss (if Dr side bigger) on disposal, transferred to the Profit and Loss Account.
Worked Example
A machine that cost ₦1,000,000 with accumulated depreciation of ₦750,000 (NBV ₦250,000) is sold for ₦300,000 cash.
Disposal Account: Dr Cost 1,000,000 | Cr Provision for Depreciation 750,000, Cr Cash 300,000 → Cr side total 1,050,000, Dr side 1,000,000 → Profit on disposal = ₦50,000 (transferred to P&L as income).

13. Bad Debts & Provision for Doubtful Debts

13.1 Bad Debts

Definition
A Bad Debt is a debt owed to the business that is considered irrecoverable and is written off as a loss.
Journal entry: Dr Bad Debts Account; Cr Debtor's Account (removing them from the books)
At year end: Dr Profit & Loss Account; Cr Bad Debts Account (transferring the expense)

13.2 Provision for Doubtful Debts (Allowance for Doubtful Debts)

Applying the Prudence concept (Section 2.9), a business estimates that some of its remaining debtors (not yet identified as definitely bad) may fail to pay, and creates a provision — a general "cushion" against likely future losses — usually a percentage of the outstanding debtors after bad debts have already been removed.

First-time creation: Dr Profit & Loss Account; Cr Provision for Doubtful Debts Account
Increase in provision (later year): Dr P&L Account with the INCREASE only; Cr Provision Account
Decrease in provision (later year): Dr Provision Account with the DECREASE only; Cr P&L Account (as income)
Worked Example
Debtors at year end total ₦800,000. A specific debtor owing ₦50,000 is now confirmed to be bad and must be written off first. Provision for doubtful debts is to be maintained at 5% of the remaining good debtors.

Step 1 — Write off bad debt: Dr Bad Debts ₦50,000; Cr Debtor ₦50,000. Remaining debtors = 800,000 − 50,000 = ₦750,000.
Step 2 — New provision required = 5% × 750,000 = ₦37,500. If the existing (opening) provision was, say, ₦20,000, the INCREASE needed is ₦17,500: Dr P&L ₦17,500; Cr Provision for Doubtful Debts ₦17,500.
Balance Sheet shows: Debtors 750,000 LESS Provision for Doubtful Debts 37,500 = Net Debtors ₦712,500.

13.3 Bad Debts Recovered

If a debt previously written off as bad is later unexpectedly paid, it is NOT simply reversed against the old debtor account. It is recorded as income: Dr Cash/Bank Account; Cr Bad Debts Recovered Account (a separate nominal/income account, credited to P&L).

Common Mistake
Many students calculate the new provision on the ORIGINAL debtors figure and forget to first deduct the newly identified bad debt(s). Always write off bad debts FIRST, then calculate the provision percentage on what's left.

14. Accruals & Prepayments

These year-end adjustments exist because of the Accrual/Matching concept (Section 2.6) — expenses and income must be recorded in the period they relate to, regardless of when cash actually changed hands.

14.1 Accrued Expenses (Expenses Owing)

An expense that relates to the current accounting period but has not yet been paid (and often not yet even billed) by the year end.

Journal entry: Dr Expense Account (P&L) with the amount owing; Cr Accrued Expenses (a current liability in the Balance Sheet)

14.2 Prepaid Expenses (Expenses Paid in Advance)

An expense already paid in cash during the current period, but which relates (wholly or partly) to a future accounting period.

Journal entry: Dr Prepaid Expenses (a current asset in the Balance Sheet); Cr Expense Account (P&L), removing the portion that doesn't belong to this period

14.3 Accrued Income (Income Receivable/Owing)

Income earned during the current period but not yet received in cash by year end (e.g. rent receivable from a tenant, still unpaid).

Journal entry: Dr Accrued Income (a current asset); Cr Income Account (P&L), adding the amount earned but not yet received

14.4 Income Received in Advance (Prepaid Income / Unearned Income)

Cash already received during the current period, but which relates (wholly or partly) to a future accounting period, so it hasn't been "earned" yet.

Journal entry: Dr Income Account (P&L), removing the unearned portion; Cr Income Received in Advance (a current liability)
Worked Example
Rent expense paid during the year (from the cash book/trial balance) = ₦600,000, for the 12 months to 31 January (i.e. one month, January, relates to next year). Prepaid rent = one month's worth = 600,000 ÷ 13 months × 1 = approx. ₦46,154 if the ₦600,000 covers 13 months paid in advance; more simply, if ₦600,000 pays rent up to 31 January next year and the accounting year ends 31 December, one month (January) is prepaid.

Rent charged to P&L this year = ₦600,000 − prepaid portion. Prepaid Rent appears in the Balance Sheet as a current asset.

14.5 Effect on Final Accounts — Quick Reference

AdjustmentEffect on P&L (this year)Effect on Balance Sheet
Accrued ExpenseIncreases the expense (add to amount paid)Current Liability
Prepaid ExpenseDecreases the expense (subtract from amount paid)Current Asset
Accrued IncomeIncreases the income (add to amount received)Current Asset
Income Received in AdvanceDecreases the income (subtract from amount received)Current Liability

15. Capital & Revenue Expenditure

Capital ExpenditureRevenue Expenditure
DefinitionSpending on acquiring, improving, or extending the life/earning capacity of a fixed assetSpending on the day-to-day running of the business, benefit used up within the current period
Where it appearsBalance Sheet (as a fixed asset), then depreciated over timeTrading / Profit & Loss Account (as an expense, in full, this period)
ExamplesBuying a motor van; cost of installing new machinery; major extension to a building; legal costs of buying propertyFuel for the van; repairs and servicing; rent, salaries, insurance, stationery

Also related: Capital Income (proceeds from selling a fixed asset, or additional capital introduced by the owner) versus Revenue Income (income from normal trading activities — sales, commission received, rent received).

Common Mistake — Effect of Misclassification
Treating capital expenditure as revenue expenditure (or vice versa) is an Error of Principle (Section 9.3) and distorts both the Profit & Loss Account (profit understated/overstated) AND the Balance Sheet (fixed assets understated/overstated) in the same period. Always ask: "does this create or improve a long-term asset, or is it just keeping the business running today?"

Exam Tip — Carriage In vs Carriage Out
Carriage Inwards (cost of transporting purchased goods TO the business) is added in the Trading Account as part of Cost of Goods Sold. Carriage Outwards (cost of delivering sold goods OUT to customers) is a selling expense in the Profit & Loss Account. Mixing these up is one of the most common ACC 101 exam errors.

16.4 Adjustments Checklist Before Preparing Final Accounts

  • Closing stock (valued at the lower of cost and net realisable value).
  • Depreciation for the year on each class of fixed asset.
  • Bad debts to write off, and the provision for doubtful debts required.
  • Accrued expenses and prepaid expenses.
  • Accrued income and income received in advance.
  • Goods taken by the owner for personal use (drawings of stock) — reduces Purchases, increases Drawings.
  • Interest on capital / interest on drawings, if applicable to the specific question.
Exam Tip
Items already in the Trial Balance are used ONCE only, in the Trading/P&L Account or Balance Sheet as appropriate. Items appearing in "Additional Information" / "Notes" below the trial balance (adjustments) are used TWICE — once in the Trading/P&L Account and once again in the Balance Sheet (e.g. closing stock appears as a deduction in the Trading Account AND as a current asset in the Balance Sheet).

17. Exam Technique & Common Mistakes

  • Always show workings. Nigerian university accounting exams award significant partial credit for correct method even when the final figure is wrong — an unsupported final answer alone can lose most of the marks.
  • Label every account and every column ("Dr"/"Cr", "₦", the account name, the date) — unlabelled figures are frequently penalized.
  • Balance every T-account you open — an account left "open" (not balanced off) usually costs marks even if the figures inside are correct.
  • Read "as at" versus "for the year ended" carefully — the Balance Sheet is "as at" a specific date (a snapshot); the Trading and P&L Account is "for the year ended" (a period).
  • Don't forget narrations in Journal entries where asked — many marking schemes allocate marks specifically for the narration.
  • Double-check your debit/credit direction before posting — a very common error is instinctively debiting income or crediting an expense under exam pressure.
  • For control accounts and reconciliations, always define which direction you are adding/subtracting — state clearly whether you're moving from Cash Book to Bank Statement or vice versa.
  • Practice past questions from your specific university/lecturer where possible, since local emphasis (e.g. more weight on bank reconciliation vs control accounts) varies, but the underlying content is what's covered above across virtually all Nigerian universities.

18. Practice Questions with Full Solutions

Q1. Define accounting and state THREE objectives of accounting. (6 marks)
Accounting is the process of identifying, recording, classifying, summarising, and communicating financial information about an economic entity to permit informed judgements and decisions by users of the information. Objectives: (i) to ascertain the profit or loss of the business over a period; (ii) to ascertain the financial position of the business at a point in time; (iii) to provide accounting information to assist users (owners, management, creditors, government, etc.) in decision-making.
Q2. State the effect of the following transactions on the Accounting Equation: (a) Bought furniture on credit, ₦150,000. (b) Owner introduces additional capital of ₦100,000 cash. (c) Paid a creditor ₦40,000 by cheque.
(a) Assets increase by 150,000 (Furniture); Liabilities increase by 150,000 (Creditor) — equation still balances, Capital unaffected.
(b) Assets increase by 100,000 (Cash); Capital increases by 100,000 — Liabilities unaffected.
(c) Assets decrease by 40,000 (Bank); Liabilities decrease by 40,000 (Creditor) — Capital unaffected.
Q3. Journalise the following: (i) Bought goods on credit from Emeka ₦65,000. (ii) Returned damaged goods worth ₦10,000 to Emeka. (iii) Paid Emeka the balance owing by cheque, having received a cash discount of ₦2,000.
(i) Dr Purchases A/c ₦65,000; Cr Emeka's A/c ₦65,000.
(ii) Dr Emeka's A/c ₦10,000; Cr Purchases Returns A/c ₦10,000.
(iii) Balance owing to Emeka now = 65,000 − 10,000 = 55,000. Cheque paid = 55,000 − 2,000 (discount) = 53,000.
Dr Emeka's A/c ₦55,000; Cr Bank A/c ₦53,000; Cr Discount Received A/c ₦2,000.
Q4. A trial balance failed to agree, the credit side exceeding the debit side by ₦15,000. A suspense account was opened. It was later discovered that: (i) The Sales Day Book was overcast (over-added) by ₦9,000. (ii) A payment of ₦6,000 for insurance was correctly entered in the cash book but posted to the Insurance Account as ₦600. Prepare the journal entries to correct these errors and show the Suspense Account.
(i) Sales overcast by 9,000 means Sales (Credit) is overstated by 9,000 → Dr Sales A/c ₦9,000; Cr Suspense A/c ₦9,000 (reducing the credit-heavy suspense balance).
(ii) Insurance posted as 600 instead of 6,000 means Insurance (Debit) is understated by 5,400 → Dr Insurance A/c ₦5,400; Cr Suspense A/c ₦5,400.

Suspense Account: Opening balance (Cr) 15,000. Add corrections posted to the credit side: from (i) 9,000 and (ii) 5,400 = 14,400 credited... Note: since both corrections credit the Suspense Account, and the original suspense balance itself was a credit of 15,000, students should re-total workings carefully against their specific trial balance figures — the key technique demonstrated is: identify which single side of each error entry is missing/wrong, and post the correction to Suspense on the OPPOSITE side to balance it out.
Q5. From the following, calculate the Cost of Goods Sold and Gross Profit: Opening Stock ₦120,000; Purchases ₦640,000; Purchases Returns ₦20,000; Carriage Inwards ₦15,000; Closing Stock ₦95,000; Sales ₦900,000; Sales Returns ₦30,000.
Net Purchases = 640,000 − 20,000 = 620,000.
Cost of Goods Available for Sale = Opening Stock 120,000 + Net Purchases 620,000 + Carriage Inwards 15,000 = 755,000.
Cost of Goods Sold = 755,000 − Closing Stock 95,000 = ₦660,000.
Net Sales = 900,000 − 30,000 = 870,000.
Gross Profit = Net Sales − Cost of Goods Sold = 870,000 − 660,000 = ₦210,000.
Q6. Distinguish between capital expenditure and revenue expenditure, giving TWO examples of each.
Capital expenditure is money spent on acquiring, improving, or extending the useful life of fixed assets, and appears in the Balance Sheet (e.g. purchase of a delivery van; cost of extending a factory building). Revenue expenditure is money spent on the day-to-day running of the business, fully consumed within the accounting period, and appears in the Profit & Loss Account (e.g. fuel for the delivery van; salaries paid to staff).

19. Glossary of Key Terms

Asset
A resource owned/controlled by the business expected to bring future economic benefit.
Liability
An amount owed by the business to an outside party.
Capital
The owner's investment/stake in the business (Assets minus Liabilities).
Debtor
A person/entity who owes money to the business (an asset to the business).
Creditor
A person/entity to whom the business owes money (a liability of the business).
Drawings
Cash or goods withdrawn from the business by the owner for personal use, reducing capital.
Trial Balance
A list of ledger balances used to check the arithmetical accuracy of double entry postings.
Gross Profit
Net Sales minus Cost of Goods Sold, before deducting operating expenses.
Net Profit
Gross Profit plus other income, minus all operating expenses.
Depreciation
The systematic allocation of a fixed asset's depreciable cost over its useful life.
Accrual
An expense incurred or income earned but not yet paid/received in cash.
Prepayment
An expense paid or income received in cash, relating to a future period.
Suspense Account
A temporary account used to hold a trial balance difference until the underlying error is found.
Control Account
A summary total account that should agree with the sum of individual balances in a subsidiary ledger.
Contra Entry
A transaction where both the debit and credit occur within the cash book itself (cash-to-bank transfers).
Net Book Value (NBV)
The cost of a fixed asset less accumulated depreciation to date.

20. Quick-Revision Summary

  • Accounting = identify → record → classify → summarise → communicate financial information.
  • 13 core concepts: Business Entity, Going Concern, Money Measurement, Historical Cost, Dual Aspect, Accrual/Matching, Realisation, Consistency, Prudence, Materiality, Objectivity, Full Disclosure, Periodicity.
  • Accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Capital — must always balance.
  • Double entry: every transaction has a debit and an equal, opposite credit.
  • Flow of records: Source Document → Book of Original Entry (Day Book/Journal/Cash Book) → Ledger → Trial Balance → Final Accounts (Trading, P&L, Balance Sheet).
  • 6 errors NOT revealed by a trial balance: omission, commission, principle, original entry, complete reversal, compensating.
  • Bank reconciliation differences: unpresented cheques, uncredited lodgements, bank charges, standing orders, dishonoured cheques, errors.
  • Depreciation methods: Straight-line (equal amount yearly) and Reducing Balance (% of NBV, front-loaded).
  • Adjustments before final accounts: closing stock, depreciation, bad debts/provision, accruals, prepayments.
  • Net Assets in the Balance Sheet must always equal Closing Capital.
